# Borrow MIT code, study GPL ideas

The open-source projects closest to Slate fall into two groups. The first is a 2026 wave of small, local-first, account-free pre-production suites: **OpenShotDesigner** (GPL-3.0), **Open Gate** (GPL-3.0), **FilmOrganizer** (MIT) and **Call Sheet Tool** (MIT). The second is a group of mature screenwriting tools: **OpenDraft** (MIT), **Beat**, **Starc** and **Scrite** (all GPL-3.0), and **afterwriting** and **Better Fountain** (both MIT). No single project covers Slate's full range, from screenplay editor through breakdown, stripboard, call sheets, storyboards, story map, insights and table read. OpenShotDesigner comes nearest on production features and OpenDraft nearest on the writing side. License decides how each can be used. Slate ships as an Electron .exe, so sharing that file counts as distribution, and copying GPL or AGPL code would force the whole app under those licenses. The highest-value sources of actual code are all permissive: afterwriting's `liner.js` pagination rules, FilmOrganizer's rule-based auto-breakdown (`rules.ts` plus `rules-dictionary.ts`, about 430 lines, no dependencies), Call Sheet Tool's vanilla-JS call-sheet patterns, and kokoro-js (Apache-2.0) for a neural table read. GPL projects are still very useful as sources of ideas, chiefly OpenShotDesigner's schedule-health checks and what-if previews, Open Gate's text-anchored tags, and Beat's FDX revision handling. Almost every production-side project is under six months old and has fewer than 60 stars. Treat them as idea sources, not dependencies.

## The 2026 local-first suites mirror Slate almost feature for feature

Slate's architecture is now common rather than unusual. **OpenShotDesigner** runs in the browser with "no account, no backend". It has a lined script, shot list, storyboard, stripboard, call sheets, crew/cast, budget, locations, a continuity log and DaVinci Resolve metadata export, which makes it the closest feature sibling ([GitHub](https://github.com/koosoli/OpenShotDesigner)). **Call Sheet Tool** is the closest architectural sibling. It is vanilla JavaScript with plain JSON data files and is MIT-licensed ([GitHub](https://github.com/sthielmann/Call-Sheet-Tool)). **Open Gate** is a React/ProseMirror PWA built on CineSched. It covers writing, breakdown in 21 categories, stripboard, Day Out of Days (DOOD), .ics export, budget and call sheets ([GitHub](https://github.com/opengateapp/opengate)). **FilmOrganizer** (MIT) ships Windows installers and imports PDF, DOCX, FDX, Fountain and TXT ([GitHub](https://github.com/nachobust/FilmOrganizer)). Nothing at the studio-tracker end overlaps meaningfully with Slate. **Kitsu** is AGPL and built for animation and VFX pipelines, not stripboards or call sheets ([Kitsu](https://github.com/cgwire/kitsu)). No open-source Movie Magic Budgeting equivalent exists; budgeting appears only as a module inside the suites.

Two of these suites model the data in ways Slate should note. Open Gate stores breakdown tags as "marks on the script text" that move with the words as the script is edited. When a revised draft is re-imported, it keeps scene ids for headings that did not change, so tags, shots and schedule placement survive ([Open Gate](https://github.com/opengateapp/opengate)). OpenShotDesigner never deletes a removed scene silently; it shows it as "SCENE 12 — OMITTED". It also takes forced scene numbers from Fountain `#8A#` or from FDX attributes ([OpenShotDesigner](https://github.com/koosoli/OpenShotDesigner)). Both behaviours deal with the core risk of an integrated app like Slate: script revisions quietly breaking downstream breakdown and schedule data.

## Screenplay engines: one MIT paginator matters most

The open-source screenwriting field is small. Among full editors, **Beat** (macOS/iOS, GPL-3), **Story Architect/Starc** (C++/Qt, GPL-3), **Scrite** (C++/Qt, GPL-3) and **Trelby** (Python, GPL-2) are maintained. **KIT Scenarist** is archived, and Starc replaces it ([starc](https://github.com/story-apps/starc)). **OpenDraft** is the closest analogue to Slate. It is a new (2026), MIT-licensed web-tech Final Draft alternative built on React 19, TipTap, a FastAPI backend and Tauri 2. It imports FDX, Fountain and PDF, offers character and scene autocomplete, and has a table read that uses on-device voices or an AI provider ([OpenDraft](https://github.com/Proteus-Technologies-Private-Limited/OpenDraft)). Its README does not mention revision colours, locked pages or dual dialogue, and its source was not inspected. **Scenarly** publishes its source but is "All rights reserved", so none of its code may be copied ([Scenarly](https://github.com/Lycoon/scenarly)).

The best piece of code to copy is afterwriting's **`liner.js`**. It is MIT, about 300 lines, and handles word wrap, page-break rules, MORE/CONT'D and splitting dual dialogue across pages ([liner.js](https://github.com/ifrost/afterwriting-labs/blob/master/js/utils/liner.js)). The rules are concrete. It never breaks after a scene heading or right before a transition. Action blocks of 1–3 lines break only after their last line, and blocks of 4 or more never break after the first or second-to-last line. It never separates a character cue or parenthetical from the dialogue that follows. A mid-speech split inserts `(MORE)` and repeats the cue with `(CONT'D)`. The matching print profile uses **55 lines per page on US Letter (57 on A4)**, Courier 12 at 6 lines per inch, and line widths of **61 characters for action, 33 for character, 26 for parenthetical and 36 for dialogue** ([print-profiles.js](https://github.com/ifrost/afterwriting-labs/blob/master/js/utils/print-profiles.js)). Better Fountain (MIT, 455 stars, active) carries a TypeScript port as `src/pdf/liner.ts` ([Better Fountain](https://github.com/piersdeseilligny/betterfountain)). OpenShotDesigner instead uses 54 lines on Letter and 58 on A4 ([OpenShotDesigner](https://github.com/koosoli/OpenShotDesigner)). That difference shows these figures are approximations of Final Draft output, not exact matches. Neither pager handles A-pages or locked pages, and **no surveyed open-source project documents either feature**. Beat is the only one that claims "Final Draft import / export, including revisions" alongside revision-generation management, and it is GPL-3 ([Beat](https://github.com/lmparppei/Beat)). Slate's locked-page and A-page logic will have to be original work.

For format conversion, the libraries are useful mainly as test oracles, not as replacements. **fountain-js** (MIT, zero dependencies, about 42k downloads a month) is the only JS Fountain parser in real current use ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/fountain-js)). **screenplay-tools** (MIT) parses and writes both Fountain and FDX in JS, Python, C# and C++, and its README lists boneyard, notes, six levels of sections, synopses, lyrics and dual dialogue. Its JS FDX API is documented only by analogy with the C# one ([screenplay-tools](https://github.com/wildwinter/screenplay-tools)). No maintained standalone JS FDX package exists on npm ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/fdx2fountain)), and FDX is plain XML, so Slate's own DOMParser-based FDX code is the right approach. For PDF import, **Featherweight** (MIT, 2026) converts screenplay PDFs to Fountain or FDX, including dual dialogue and scene numbers. It depends on a native napi binary, so it could run only in Electron's main process ([Featherweight](https://github.com/bantamhq/featherweight), [pdf-inspector](https://registry.npmjs.org/@firecrawl/pdf-inspector/latest)). **screenplay-pdf-to-json** (Python, MIT) points to three habits worth adopting: detect where front matter ends, treat dual dialogue as a distinct element type, and warn that it "works well for 'clean' PDF screenplays, not OCR PDFs" ([SMASH-CUT](https://github.com/SMASH-CUT/screenplay-pdf-to-json)). If Slate wants a direct PDF download, **jsPDF** (MIT, built-in Courier) avoids the stream shims pdfkit needs in the browser ([jsPDF](https://www.npmjs.com/package/jspdf), [pdfkit](https://www.npmjs.com/package/pdfkit)).

## Auto-breakdown is dictionaries plus CAPS, and FilmOrganizer's is portable

In practice, open-source auto-breakdown is deterministic: keyword dictionaries combined with screenplay conventions. **FilmOrganizer's** MIT implementation is the one to port. It uses one regex per category, longest terms first, with optional plurals and Unicode word boundaries. It scans only action, general, shot and scene-heading paragraphs, never dialogue. It detects character introductions such as "MARTA (60s)" or "TOMÁS, 30", and skips V.O. and NARRATOR cues ([rules.ts](https://github.com/nachobust/FilmOrganizer/blob/main/src/rules.ts)). Two product ideas come with it. Every suggestion records the source quote and the rule that produced it, so auto-tags can be audited. And an element confirmed once is spread to "every other scene that mentions it". The tool also flags production risks such as night exteriors, minors, water, weapons and crowds ([FilmOrganizer](https://github.com/nachobust/FilmOrganizer)). ScriptBreak (Apache-2.0) adds the second standard heuristic, tagging "from the CAPS the writer used in action lines". It also seeds starter shot lists from called shots such as CLOSE ON, INSERT and POV ([ScriptBreak](https://github.com/wassermanproductions/scriptbreak)).

Machine learning adds little here. **No public dataset labels screenplay props, wardrobe or vehicles**. A browser-runnable NER model such as Xenova/bert-base-NER only emits generic PER/ORG/LOC/MISC labels ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/Xenova/bert-base-NER)), and character cues already find people more reliably. The useful lesson from research is reconciliation. The STAGE knowledge-graph paper reports that single-pass extraction "exhibits omissions, duplicates, and invalid relations" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08510v1)). For Slate, the cheap version of that fix is alias merging in the element manager, for example treating "gun" and "pistol" as one element. For category design, Open Gate's 21 categories and their highlight colours are a ready-made default set ([defaults.ts](https://github.com/opengateapp/opengate/blob/main/src/model/defaults.ts)). The Universal Category Identification scheme gives stable numeric IDs (scene categories 0–6, action categories from 100) that survive renaming ([UCI](https://github.com/UniversalScheduleStandard/UniversalCategoryIdentification)). UCI is CC BY-NC-ND, so implement the IDs without redistributing the spec text.

## Scheduling tools win on feedback, not optimisation

**None of the inspected open-source production apps ships an automatic schedule optimiser.** Instead they give computed feedback on manual drag-and-drop, and that is where the ideas worth borrowing are. OpenShotDesigner's `health.ts` flags `cast_split_across_locations`, `cast_unavailable`, `company_moves`, `day_overruns`, `distant_locations` and `short_turnaround`. Its `WhatIfPanel` shows, before a strip is dropped, the effect on both days' pages and time and on cast paid and hold days ([OpenShotDesigner scheduling](https://github.com/koosoli/OpenShotDesigner/tree/main/src/domain/scheduling)). Open Gate's `engine.ts`, ported from CineSched, splits the work into `cascade()` (where fixed start times reset the clock), `dayTotals()`, `scanConflicts()` and a lock diff ([engine.ts](https://github.com/opengateapp/opengate/blob/main/src/schedule/engine.ts)). Both are GPL, so reimplement these from their descriptions; do not copy the code. Formal optimisation exists only in academic work on the talent scheduling problem (CSPLib prob039), which minimises cast idle cost over scene orderings ([CSPLib](https://www.csplib.org/Problems/prob039/references/)). Exact solvers are exponential, so a realistic Slate optimiser would be greedy. It would cluster strips by location and day/night, pack days up to a page-eighths cap, then improve the result with pairwise swaps scored on the same terms the health checks flag.

Two details are worth taking. Strip colours conflict between projects. OpenShotDesigner uses EXT/day yellow, INT/night blue and EXT/night green ([stripboardPrint.ts](https://github.com/koosoli/OpenShotDesigner/blob/main/src/domain/scheduling/stripboardPrint.ts)). Open Gate and CineSched swap the two night colours and add Dawn, Dusk and Afternoon slots ([defaults.ts](https://github.com/opengateapp/opengate/blob/main/src/model/defaults.ts)). Slate should therefore make strip colours configurable. For call sheets, Call Sheet Tool (MIT) is the model to copy. It computes every time from durations and one start time ("you never type clock times"). It also offers "Push to next day", "Shift all times", "Add everyone with tag", the industry revision-colour rotation (white, blue, pink, yellow, green, goldenrod, buff, salmon, cherry), and weather and sunrise lookups fetched only on click ([Call Sheet Tool](https://github.com/sthielmann/Call-Sheet-Tool)). For Movie Magic interchange, open-movie-schedule (MIT) reports that MMS 10 imports Screenwriter XML and legacy `.sex` files, and that FDX tags are lost on import ([open-movie-schedule](https://github.com/sfingali/open-movie-schedule)). Stripboard Studio's own README admits that its first MMSX export "was rejected by MMS6/MMS10" ([Stripboard Studio](https://github.com/nebelwerfer41/stripboard-studio)), which shows how fragile that interop is.

## Storyboards, story map and table read have clean upgrade paths

**Storyboarder** (Wonder Unit, about 3.9k stars) is an Electron/JS app like Slate and remains the reference storyboard tool. Its license is reportedly MIT with exceptions that bar commercial resale, which makes it non-OSI ([Linux Magazine](https://www.linux-magazine.com/Issues/2018/206/FOSSPicks/(offset)/12)). Reimplement its concepts rather than copying source. Its feature set gives Slate a short list to borrow: thirds, centre and onion-skin guides, printable blank worksheets, a configurable PDF sheet, and animated GIF and Premiere/Final Cut XML timeline export ([Storyboarder](https://github.com/wonderunit/storyboarder), [Wonder Unit](https://www.wonderunit.com/storyboarder)). Each of these can be built with canvas, `window.print()`, `MediaRecorder` and plain text generation, with no new dependencies. Local AI storyboard generation such as StoryDiffusion needs a 24 GB-class GPU and a Python stack ([StoryDiffusion](https://github.com/hvision-nku/storydiffusion)). That makes it suitable only as an off-by-default "send prompt to my local ComfyUI" hook.

There is no maintained open-source screenplay-analytics app, only notebooks and research code. Their methods port directly to Slate's Insights and Story Map: a scene-by-character co-occurrence graph ([Film_Script_Analysis](https://nbviewer.org/format/script/github/AdeboyeML/Film_Script_Analysis/blob/e2fec93a9fa163b49893788b46cecac29c51078a/American-Pie_Movie_Analysis.ipynb)), and per-line lexicon sentiment smoothed over the script. The "Emotion Dynamics in Movie Dialogues" paper models valence, arousal and dominance per character, and arousal is a natural automatic proxy for Slate's hand-set intensity curve ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01345v5.pdf)). The NRC lexicons behind that work have restricted commercial terms, so check the license before bundling them.

For the table read, **kokoro-js** is the clear upgrade over `speechSynthesis`. Code and weights are Apache-2.0. The q8 model is **about 86–92 MB** and provides **28 English voices** (US and UK, male and female), and it runs on WASM or WebGPU in the renderer ([Kokoro ONNX](https://huggingface.co/onnx-community/Kokoro-82M-v1.0-ONNX), [kokoro-js](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/kokoro-js@1.2.0/README.md)). One model serving every voice suits a multi-character read better than Piper's one file per voice. Piper's engine has also moved to GPL-3.0 (piper1-gpl), and each Piper voice carries its own dataset license ([rhasspy/piper](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper), [Debian WNPP](https://www.mail-archive.com/debian-wnpp@lists.debian.org/msg274337.html)). **sherpa-onnx** (Apache-2.0) is the alternative for the Electron main process, offering native speed and the widest model choice, at the cost of a native addon ([sherpa-onnx](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx)). A sensible design is to download Kokoro lazily on first use and keep OS voices as the zero-download fallback.

## Conclusion

Slate is not behind the open-source field. It is one of the few projects that combines a serious screenplay editor with a production back end; the others split along that line, with OpenDraft and Beat on the writing side and OpenShotDesigner and Open Gate on the production side. That means most gains will come from adopting specific rules and behaviours, not whole libraries. Four of them are worth adding: afterwriting's break rules, FilmOrganizer's auditable and propagating auto-tags, OpenShotDesigner-style schedule health and what-if deltas, and Kokoro voices per character. The largest remaining gap is revision-safe data. Locked pages, A-pages, and tags that survive a re-imported draft have no permissive reference implementation anywhere, so this is where Slate's original work will matter most.

The licensing rule is simple, and it matters because of the .exe. Copy code with its notice kept from MIT and Apache sources: afterwriting, Better Fountain, OpenDraft, FilmOrganizer, Call Sheet Tool, ScriptBreak, screenplay-tools, fountain-js and kokoro-js. Reimplement ideas only from GPL and AGPL sources: Beat, Starc, Scrite, Trelby, OpenShotDesigner, Open Gate, CineSched and Kitsu. Avoid Scenarly entirely. Several facts here are unverified. OpenShotDesigner's and CineSched's GPL status rests on README badges, afterwriting's MIT grant appears only in its README, and Storyboarder's exact license text is unconfirmed. Recheck these before copying anything that lies near those boundaries.
